# Humanizer

This flavor removes recurring machine-written habits while preserving the source's facts and voice. It favors specific, natural prose over language that is polished but generic.

Removing the listed patterns is half the work. Sterile, voiceless prose is its own tell, so the other half is leaving a person behind in the writing.

## Keep the facts, reshape the prose

- Keep every supported fact, name, number, date, quotation, and citation. Do not invent specificity to make a sentence sound better. A vague claim becomes a specific one only when the specific already exists in the source or is supplied by the author.
- Depth does not have to be uniform. Compress the dull parts, dwell where a person would dwell, and merge or split paragraphs freely.
- When keeping the information and mirroring the original's shape pull in different directions, the information wins.
- Preserve formatting that helps a reader scan or find things. Do not preserve shape for its own sake.

## Match the writer

- A supplied writing sample outranks every style rule here, including the dash rule in 14. Matching the author beats scrubbing the tell.
- An author's habits live in sentence lengths, vocabulary, paragraph openings, punctuation, recurring phrases, and transitions. Casual words are not upgraded and deliberate quirks are not regularized.
- Specific details, genuine uncertainty, quotations, titles, and meaningful structure all survive the pass.
- Absent a sample, the defaults described here apply.

## Voice and personality

This section applies when the content and the author's voice call for it: blog posts, essays, opinion, personal writing. For encyclopedic, technical, legal, or reference text, neutral and plain is the correct human voice, so do not add opinions, humor, or first person there.

Where voice does belong:

- Avoid uniform sentence structures, bloodless neutrality, and perfect organization.
- Let the writer hold opinions, uncertainty, mixed feelings, humor, asides, and uneven rhythm.
- Opinions and reactions are voice, not facts. Stance may be added; a factual claim may not be added to manufacture personality.

## Content patterns

1. **Inflated significance.** Cut claims that turn a routine fact into a major moment, legacy, or broad social trend without evidence.
   - Watch: stands as, serves as, is a testament to, is a reminder of, a vital / crucial / pivotal / key role, underscores its importance, reflects broader, symbolizing its enduring, contributing to the, setting the stage for, marking a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, focal point, indelible mark, deeply rooted.
2. **Performative notability.** Do not use media lists, follower counts, awards, or vague praise to imply importance when they do not explain the subject. When the source gives real context for one citation, keep that one and drop the rest of the list. Do not invent the context to justify keeping it.
   - Watch: independent coverage, local / regional / national media outlets, written by a leading expert, active social media presence.
3. **Decorative `-ing` clauses.** Remove trailing participial phrases that pretend to add analysis but only restate or embellish the sentence.
   - Watch: highlighting, underscoring, emphasizing, ensuring, reflecting, symbolizing, contributing to, cultivating, fostering, encompassing, showcasing.
4. **Promotional description.** Replace advertising adjectives and grand claims with concrete description.
   - Watch: boasts a, vibrant, rich (figurative), profound, enhancing its, showcasing, exemplifies, commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking (figurative), renowned, breathtaking, must-visit, stunning.
5. **Vague attribution.** Name a source when one is available. Otherwise state the fact plainly or omit an unsupported claim instead of crediting unnamed experts, critics, or reports. Never invent a source to make a sentence look sourced.
   - Watch: industry reports, observers have cited, experts argue, some critics argue, several sources, several publications (when few are cited).
6. **Template future material.** Remove generic "challenges," "legacy," or "future outlook" sections when they add no concrete information. The specifics that would rescue such a section come from the sources, never from the edit itself.
   - Watch: despite its ... faces several challenges, despite these challenges, Challenges and Legacy, Future Outlook.

## Language and grammar patterns

7. **AI-coded vocabulary.** Avoid clusters of elevated, abstract, or overused words when ordinary wording says the same thing. These words tend to co-occur, so a cluster is the signal rather than any single word.
   - Watch: actually, additionally, align with, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (verb), interplay, intricate, intricacies, key (adjective), landscape (abstract), pivotal, showcase, tapestry (abstract), testament, underscore (verb), valuable, vibrant.
8. **Copula avoidance.** Prefer _is_, _are_, _has_, and _have_ over ceremonial substitutes.
   - Watch: serves as, stands as, marks, represents, boasts, features, offers.
9. **Negative parallelism.** Replace constructions such as "not only ... but also" and "it is not just X, it is Y" with one direct statement. The same applies to clipped tailing negations such as "no guessing" or "no wasted motion" tacked onto a sentence instead of written as a real clause.
10. **Forced threes.** Do not group ideas into threes merely to sound complete. Often all three items are filler and the sentence should go.
11. **Synonym cycling.** Reuse the accurate term instead of changing words only to avoid repetition.
12. **False ranges.** Do not frame unrelated examples as a journey from one thing to another.
13. **Hidden actors and fragments.** Name the actor when it clarifies the sentence. Avoid subjectless fragments and passive voice that hides a necessary actor.

## Style patterns

14. **Em and en dashes.** The style admits none, which makes this a hard constraint rather than a preference. Each one gives way, in rough order of preference, to a period, a comma, a colon, parentheses, or a rewrite. The disguised forms count too, including a spaced em dash and a double hyphen standing in for one. A supplied writing sample that uses dashes overrides this rule at the sample's own frequency.
15. **Mechanical bolding.** Use bold only when it carries real meaning.
16. **Label-first lists.** Avoid lists made from bold mini-headings followed by a sentence when a simple list or paragraph is clearer.
17. **Title case headings.** Use sentence case for headings unless the established style requires title case.
18. **Decorative emoji.** Do not add emoji to headings or list items unless the source style calls for them.
19. **Quotation mark defaults.** Prefer straight quotation marks in technical prose unless the document's established style uses typographic quotes. Do not treat quotation style alone as a defect.

## Communication patterns

20. **Chatbot residue.** Remove canned acknowledgments, offers to continue, and other conversation artifacts that do not belong in the finished prose.
    - Watch: I hope this helps, Of course, Certainly, you're absolutely right, would you like, want me to, should I continue, let me know, here is a.
21. **Cutoff disclaimers and invented gaps.** Do not fill missing knowledge with speculation or with a paragraph about unavailable information. State what is known, say plainly that a source does not cover it, or omit it. Watch for the stock filler that models reach for when a subject is not documented.
    - Watch: as of [date], up to my last training update, while specific details are limited, while specific details are scarce, based on available information, not publicly available, maintains a low profile, keeps personal details private, prefers to stay out of the spotlight, likely grew up, likely studied, it is believed that.
22. **Servile tone.** Remove unearned praise, agreement, or people-pleasing language.
    - Watch: great question, you're absolutely right, that's an excellent point.

## Filler and framing patterns

23. **Filler phrases.** Replace padded lead-ins with the direct statement.
    - Watch: in order to, due to the fact that, at this point in time, in the event that, has the ability to, it is important to note that.
24. **Excessive hedging.** Keep necessary uncertainty, but remove stacked qualifiers that weaken a clear claim.
    - Watch: could potentially, may possibly, might have some, it could be argued that.
25. **Generic positive endings.** End on the last concrete fact instead of a broad optimistic send-off. When the source states real plans, use those instead.
    - Watch: the future looks bright, exciting times lie ahead, a step in the right direction, journey toward.
26. **Hyphenated pairs.** Keep the hyphen when the compound modifies a following noun. Drop it when the compound follows the noun, where writers usually leave it out.
    - Watch: third-party, cross-functional, client-facing, data-driven, decision-making, well-known, high-quality, real-time, long-term, end-to-end.
27. **Authority tropes.** Remove phrases that claim to reveal a deeper truth when they only restate an ordinary point with extra ceremony.
    - Watch: the real question is, at its core, in reality, what really matters, fundamentally, the deeper issue, the heart of the matter.
28. **Signposting.** Do not announce an explanation before giving it.
    - Watch: let's dive in, let's explore, let's break this down, here's what you need to know, now let's look at, without further ado.
29. **Fragmented headers.** Remove a heading's empty warm-up sentence when the useful content can begin immediately.
30. **Change narration.** Describe the current thing, not the fact that it replaced an earlier implementation, unless the document is version-scoped: a changelog, release notes, or a migration guide.
31. **Manufactured punchlines and staccato drama.** Do not write every sentence to land like a quotable closer, and do not stack short declarative fragments to create emphasis. One short sentence for emphasis is fine; a run of them sounds engineered.
32. **Aphorism formulas.** Replace slogan-like metaphors with the concrete claim they are gesturing at.
    - Watch: X is the Y of Z, X becomes a trap, X is not a tool but a mirror, the language of, the currency of, the architecture of.
33. **Rhetorical openers.** Remove staged candid hooks and fake-candid pauses before an ordinary point. The tell is the theatrical pause and reveal, not the word itself.
    - Watch: Honestly?, Look, Here's the thing, The thing is, Let's be honest, Real talk (as standalone hooks).

## Do not overcorrect

A careful human writer can hit several of the patterns above with no machine involvement. None of the following is a reliable indicator on its own.

- **Perfect grammar and consistent style.** Many writers are professionals or have been edited. Polish is not evidence.
- **Mixed casual and formal registers.** This often marks a person in a technical field, a young writer, or someone with neurodivergent prose habits.
- **Bland or robotic prose.** The patterns above are specific. Dryness without them is just dry writing.
- **Formal or academic vocabulary.** Pattern 7 watches particular overused words, not every elevated word. Leave _ostensibly_ and _constituent_ alone.
- **Letter-style openings and closings.** Salutations and sign-offs predate chatbots by centuries.
- **Common transition words in isolation.** One _however_ is not a tell. A pile of them is.
- **Typographic quotes alone.** Most editors and content systems curl quotes automatically.
- **Dashes alone.** Many editors and journalists use them heavily.
- **One short emphatic sentence.** Flag staccato drama only when several fragments run together and inflate the tone.
- **"Honestly" or "look" mid-sentence.** Ordinary in casual writing. The standalone theatrical opener is the tell.
- **Unsourced claims.** Most writing is unsourced.
- **Correct, complex formatting.** Templates and visual editors produce clean output on their own.
- **Secondhand text.** Do not rewrite a watched phrase inside a quotation, title, proper name, or example where the phrase is being discussed rather than used.

When in doubt, look for a cluster of these patterns rather than an isolated one.

## Signs of human writing

Lean toward leaving the prose alone where these appear, because over-editing destroys what makes a piece sound human.

- **Specific, unusual, hard-to-fabricate detail.** A real address, a strange quotation, an aside about the lawyer who used to work upstairs from the writer's dentist. Machines round specifics off; people hoard them.
- **Mixed feelings and unresolved tension.** "I think this is mostly good, but it bothers me and I cannot say why."
- **Dated, era-bound references.** Slang, memes, or in-jokes that map to a specific year and subculture.
- **Editorial choices the writer can defend.** A cut or a word the author can explain is a strong human signal.
- **Variety in sentence length.** Real writing alternates short and long rather than settling into an even mid-length cadence.
- **Genuine asides, parentheticals, and self-corrections.** Interruptions like "(I keep wanting to write _almost_ here, but it really was certain.)"
